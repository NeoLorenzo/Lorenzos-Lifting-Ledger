# Resistance Training Outcome Study Selection & Quality Control Protocol

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how the project selects, evaluates, weights, and interprets resistance-training studies that measure longitudinal outcomes.

The objective is not to treat all published studies as equally informative. Resistance-training research varies substantially in population, intervention duration, measurement quality, training control, statistical power, adherence, and reporting quality. A published result should therefore only influence the project's recommendations in proportion to the quality and applicability of the underlying evidence.

This protocol is designed primarily for hypertrophy research, although many of the general quality-control principles also apply to other chronic resistance-training outcomes.

The system should prioritize:

1. **Internal validity** — did the study actually test what it claims to test?
2. **Outcome validity** — was the relevant adaptation measured accurately?
3. **Applicability** — does the population resemble the lifters to whom the recommendation will apply?
4. **Intervention fidelity** — did participants actually perform the intended training?
5. **Statistical credibility** — does the analysis support the conclusion?
6. **Replication** — is the finding supported independently?

A study should not receive high evidential weight merely because it is randomized, peer reviewed, statistically significant, frequently cited, or included in a meta-analysis.

## 2. Scope

This protocol applies to studies measuring chronic outcomes following a resistance-training intervention.

Examples include:

- Muscle hypertrophy
- Muscle or regional muscle size
- Strength
- Muscular endurance
- Long-term performance adaptations

For hypertrophy claims, the preferred outcome is a direct morphological measurement of the relevant muscle or muscle region.

The following are outside the scope of this protocol when evaluating hypertrophy outcomes:

- Acute EMG
- Acute muscle protein synthesis
- Acute hormonal responses
- Muscle pump
- Acute swelling
- Muscle soreness
- Creatine kinase
- Lactate
- Acute performance changes
- Biomechanical analysis without longitudinal outcomes

These forms of evidence may be useful elsewhere in the project but should not be entered into the outcome-study evidence pool.

---

## 3. Core principle: evidence is weighted, not binary

Studies should generally not be classified simply as “valid” or “invalid.” Instead, methodological limitations reduce the amount of confidence placed in their findings.

There are therefore two types of methodological rules.

### Hard exclusion rules

A study failing one of these criteria should ordinarily not be used to establish the relevant outcome claim.

### Downgrade rules

The study can still provide evidence, but its influence must be reduced.

A collection of weak studies should not automatically become strong evidence simply because many weak studies point in the same direction.

---

## 4. Define the claim before evaluating the study

Every evidence assessment must begin with a clearly specified claim.

For example:

> Training a muscle twice per week produces more hypertrophy than training it once per week when weekly volume is equated.

is different from:

> Training a muscle twice per week produces more hypertrophy than training it once per week under realistic programming conditions.

The first question attempts to isolate frequency itself. The second compares two complete training strategies. The required experimental controls differ depending on the question.

Before assessing a study, record:

- Population
- Intervention
- Comparator
- Outcome
- Duration
- Variable being investigated
- Variables that should therefore have been controlled

A study should only be used as evidence for the question it actually tested.

---

## 5. Population and training status

### 5.1 Trained populations are strongly preferred

For recommendations intended for experienced lifters, studies conducted exclusively in resistance-training novices should receive substantially reduced weight.

Novices differ from trained lifters because they:

- Respond rapidly to almost any reasonable resistance-training stimulus.
- Have substantially more room for adaptation.
- May experience large adaptations from relatively unsophisticated programming.
- May respond differently to training volume, frequency, exercise selection, and effort.
- Are more susceptible to novelty-related effects during the beginning of an intervention.

A novice-only study therefore has limited external validity for optimization questions involving trained lifters.

### Training-status classification

**High applicability**

Participants have a clearly documented history of consistent resistance training, preferably at least approximately one year, involving the muscles or movement patterns under investigation.

**Moderate applicability**

Participants are described as resistance trained, but training history is incompletely documented.

**Low applicability**

Mixed trained and untrained populations without useful subgroup analysis.

**Very low applicability**

Exclusively untrained participants.

### Project rule

Novice-only studies should not normally serve as primary evidence for optimization recommendations aimed at trained lifters. They may provide supporting evidence when higher-applicability evidence is unavailable.

Training status is principally an applicability limitation, not proof that the experiment itself was poorly conducted.

---

## 6. Study duration

Hypertrophy is a slow adaptation and small differences between competent resistance-training programs may take substantial time to become detectable.

### Preferred duration

| Duration | Classification | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **16+ weeks** | Strong | Provides substantial opportunity for meaningful differences to accumulate, assuming good adherence. |
| **12–15 weeks** | Good | Normal minimum duration for studies intended to meaningfully influence hypertrophy recommendations. |
| **8–11 weeks** | Weak-to-moderate | Can provide useful evidence but should be downgraded, particularly in trained populations. |
| **6–7 weeks** | Very weak | Should rarely affect a substantive hypertrophy recommendation. |
| **<6 weeks** | Normally exclude | Normally unsuitable for comparative hypertrophy conclusions. |

### Important qualification

Duration must not be evaluated in isolation.

A high-quality 10-week trial with excellent measurement, supervision, adherence, and experimental control can be more useful than a poorly controlled 16-week trial.

Training exposure should also be considered. Twelve weeks of once-weekly training and twelve weeks of three-times-weekly training represent very different numbers of training exposures.

The 12-week threshold is therefore a strong quality preference rather than an automatic validity boundary.

## 7. Hypertrophy measurement quality

Measurement quality is one of the most important domains in the entire framework.

MRI is generally considered the reference method for assessing changes in muscle cross-sectional area and volume, while appropriately conducted B-mode ultrasound can provide valid measures of local hypertrophy. Ultrasound muscle thickness, however, should not automatically be interpreted as whole-muscle volume because hypertrophy can occur regionally. **[PubMed citation required]**

### Measurement hierarchy

#### Tier 1 — Very strong

- MRI whole-muscle volume
- MRI multiple-site cross-sectional area
- High-quality CT morphological measurement where appropriate

#### Tier 2 — Strong

- High-quality ultrasound measuring multiple standardized regions
- Ultrasound cross-sectional area
- Carefully standardized repeated ultrasound measurements

#### Tier 3 — Moderate

- Single-site muscle-thickness ultrasound
- Muscle-fibre cross-sectional area from biopsy when used specifically to make fibre-level claims

#### Tier 4 — Weak for muscle-specific hypertrophy

- Regional DXA lean mass
- Whole-body lean mass
- Other body-composition estimates

#### Tier 5 — Normally unsuitable for hypertrophy claims

- Circumference alone
- Bioelectrical impedance
- Bodyweight-derived inference
- Visual assessment

DXA and similar measurements can identify changes in lean tissue but should not normally be treated as equivalent to directly measuring hypertrophy of a specific muscle.

---

## 8. Regional hypertrophy

Muscles do not necessarily grow uniformly.

Resistance training can produce different adaptations at proximal, middle, and distal regions of the same muscle. Research comparing training interventions increasingly recognizes this regional specificity. **[PubMed citation required]**

Therefore, a measurement at one site should not automatically be generalized to the entire muscle.

If a study measures only one location:

- The result may be used for that measured region.
- Claims about whole-muscle hypertrophy should be downgraded.
- Exercise comparisons should be interpreted particularly cautiously where regional hypertrophy is plausible.

Multiple measurement sites or whole-muscle volume should receive greater weight.

---

## 9. Measurement standardization

Even a theoretically strong measurement method can produce weak evidence if implemented poorly.

Prefer studies that standardize:

- Measurement site
- Body position
- Joint position
- Equipment
- Examiner
- Time of day
- Hydration conditions
- Recent food intake where relevant
- Recent exercise
- Image-analysis procedure

Measurement location should ideally be marked or otherwise reproducibly identified between pre- and post-testing.

The assessor should preferably be blinded to participant group or condition where practical.

Reliability data, repeated measurements, or reported measurement error increase confidence.

---

## 10. Avoid contamination from acute swelling

Post-training muscle size can temporarily increase because of fluid shifts, hyperaemia, glycogen-associated water, inflammation, and other acute responses.

Ultrasound-measured muscle thickness can remain acutely elevated following training, demonstrating why measurement timing matters. **[PubMed citation required]**

Post-intervention hypertrophy testing should therefore occur sufficiently removed from the final training session.

### Preferred rule

Approximately **48–72+ hours** after the final training session under standardized conditions.

More caution may be necessary following unusually damaging training or unfamiliar eccentric exercise.

If measurements were performed immediately after training or under poorly standardized conditions, hypertrophy evidence should be substantially downgraded.

---

## 11. Experimental design

### Strong designs

Prefer:

- Randomized controlled designs
- Randomized comparison groups
- Randomized within-subject designs where appropriate

Randomization reduces systematic differences between conditions and remains an important protection against bias. Transparent reporting of trial design, randomization, analysis, participant flow, and outcomes is central to modern CONSORT recommendations. **[PubMed citation required]**

### Within-subject designs

Split-body or contralateral designs can be particularly valuable for exercise-comparison research because each participant serves as their own control.

This greatly reduces between-person variability in hypertrophic responsiveness.

However, these designs require:

- Proper randomization of conditions to limbs.
- Statistical analysis that accounts for paired observations.
- An intervention suitable for unilateral comparison.

Within-subject designs are not automatically superior; they are simply highly useful when appropriate.

---

## 12. Isolation of the independent variable

A study can only strongly support a causal claim when the manipulated variable is sufficiently isolated.

If a study claims to test training frequency but the groups also meaningfully differ in:

- Weekly volume
- Proximity to failure
- Exercise selection
- Range of motion
- Rest periods
- Repetition tempo
- Training load
- Progression
- Exercise order

then the study does not cleanly isolate frequency.

The same principle applies to every research question.

### Project rule

If multiple meaningful training variables systematically differ between groups, the study:

- May still compare the overall programs.
- Should not be used as strong evidence that one individual variable caused the difference.

---

## 13. Do not automatically require volume equating

Whether volume should be equated depends on the research question.

If asking:

> Does frequency independently affect hypertrophy?

weekly volume should generally be controlled.

If asking:

> Does this programming strategy produce more hypertrophy in practice?

and the strategy itself allows participants to tolerate or perform more productive training volume, forcibly equalizing volume could remove part of the treatment effect.

The evaluator must therefore distinguish between:

- Isolating a variable.
- Comparing complete interventions.

---

## 14. Training effort and proximity to failure

Effort must be reasonably comparable unless effort itself is the independent variable.

Identical repetitions or loads do not guarantee identical effort. For example:

- 8 repetitions with a 9RM
- 8 repetitions with a 15RM

represent very different training stimuli.

Prefer studies that report or control:

- Repetitions in reserve
- Repetition failure
- Repetition maximum
- Velocity loss
- Another defensible measure of training effort

If one condition systematically trains considerably closer to failure than another, this becomes a major confounder for most other training-variable comparisons.

---

## 15. Training intervention reporting

A study must describe the intervention well enough to understand what participants actually performed.

Modern resistance-exercise reporting recommendations specifically emphasize exercise selection, program structure, progression/regression, exercise setting, and differences between planned and actual training. **[PubMed citation required]**

Important variables include:

- Exercises
- Sets
- Repetitions
- Load
- Relative load
- Frequency
- Range of motion
- Rest intervals
- Tempo where relevant
- Proximity to failure
- Exercise order
- Progression method
- Deloading or adjustments
- Session supervision
- Completed training volume
- Missed sessions

A protocol that says what participants were supposed to do without reporting what they actually completed is weaker evidence.

---

## 16. Supervision

Supervised training is strongly preferred.

Supervision improves confidence that:

- The prescribed exercise was actually performed.
- Range of motion was consistent.
- Effort targets were followed.
- Loads and repetitions were recorded accurately.
- Technique remained reasonably standardized.
- Progression occurred as intended.

### Classification

| Classification | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Strong** | Most or all sessions directly supervised. |
| **Moderate** | Partial supervision with good training records. |
| **Weak** | Primarily unsupervised with limited verification. |

Unsupervised studies are not automatically invalid, but uncertainty regarding intervention fidelity should reduce evidential weight.

---

## 17. Adherence

A theoretically excellent intervention is not informative if participants do not perform it.

Studies should report:

- Session attendance
- Completed sets/repetitions
- Protocol deviations
- Participant withdrawals
- Reasons for withdrawal

### Suggested thresholds

| Session adherence | Classification |
| ---: | --- |
| **≥90%** | Strong |
| **80–89%** | Acceptable |
| **70–79%** | Significant concern |
| **<70%** | Very weak unless there is a compelling reason to retain the study |

Large or systematically different adherence between groups is particularly concerning.

---

## 18. Attrition

Participant dropout can bias results, especially if withdrawal is related to the intervention.

Assess:

- Total attrition
- Attrition by condition
- Reasons for dropout
- Whether dropout differs systematically between groups
- How missing data were handled

A study where one condition loses substantially more participants should be downgraded even if the final sample appears statistically analyzable.

---

## 19. Nutrition

Nutrition can meaningfully influence hypertrophy and must therefore be considered.

Studies do not need to provide every meal, but preferably assess or control:

- Protein intake
- Total energy intake
- Major dietary changes
- Supplement use
- Body-mass changes

Particular concern should arise when groups systematically differ in protein intake, energy balance, supplementation, or weight change.

The smaller the observed hypertrophy difference between interventions, the more important these possible confounders become.

---

## 20. Supplements and performance-enhancing drugs

If the target recommendation concerns natural lifters:

- Anabolic-androgenic steroid users should normally be excluded.
- Pharmacologically enhanced samples should not be generalized to natural populations.
- Major supplementation differences between conditions must be controlled.

Common supplements used equally across groups do not necessarily reduce study quality.

Supplementation that differs systematically between conditions may confound the training comparison.

---

## 21. Population specificity

Evidence should be tagged according to:

- Sex
- Age
- Training status
- Athletic background
- Health status
- Energy balance
- Natural/enhanced status

Findings should not automatically be generalized beyond the population studied.

Examples include:

- Young men → older adults
- Men → women
- Novices → advanced lifters
- Healthy participants → rehabilitating populations
- Energy surplus → aggressive calorie deficit

Lack of perfect population matching does not invalidate a study. It limits its external validity.

---

## 22. Sample size and statistical precision

Small samples are common in resistance-training research and should be treated cautiously.

The primary concern is not an arbitrary sample-size threshold but whether the study can estimate the effect with useful precision.

Prefer:

- A priori power calculations
- Adequate final sample size
- Confidence intervals
- Effect-size estimates
- Transparent reporting of uncertainty

Small studies with wide confidence intervals should be interpreted as uncertain even when their results reach statistical significance.

A very large estimated effect from a very small sample should not automatically be treated as strong evidence.

---

## 23. Between-condition comparisons are mandatory for superiority claims

This is a critical project rule.

The following reasoning is invalid:

> Group A significantly increased muscle size. Group B did not significantly increase muscle size. Therefore Group A was superior.

The correct question is whether the difference between Group A and Group B is supported.

For superiority claims, require an appropriate:

- Group × time interaction
- Between-group change comparison
- Paired condition comparison
- Equivalent statistical analysis

Separate within-group significance tests cannot establish that groups differ from each other.

---

## 24. Statistical non-significance does not establish equivalence

`p > 0.05` means that the study did not obtain sufficient evidence against the null hypothesis under the specified statistical model.

It does not demonstrate that two interventions are equivalent.

Therefore:

> No statistically significant difference was found.

must not automatically become:

> The interventions produce the same hypertrophy.

Claims of meaningful equivalence require sufficiently precise estimates, appropriate confidence intervals, equivalence testing, or another suitable inferential framework.

---

## 25. Effect sizes and uncertainty matter more than binary significance

The project should extract, where possible:

- Mean changes
- Between-condition differences
- Effect sizes
- Confidence or credible intervals
- Sample sizes

A small but statistically significant difference may have negligible practical importance.

Conversely, a potentially meaningful effect with substantial uncertainty should be classified as uncertain, not simply “no effect.”

The project's language should distinguish:

- Evidence of benefit
- Evidence of little or no meaningful difference
- Inconclusive evidence

---

## 26. Baseline differences

Randomization does not guarantee identical baseline values, particularly in small samples.

Check whether groups substantially differ before intervention in:

- Muscle size
- Strength
- Training experience
- Body mass
- Relevant dietary variables
- Other important characteristics

Where meaningful baseline differences exist, the statistical model should appropriately account for them.

Large unexplained baseline imbalances reduce confidence.

---

## 27. Multiple comparisons

Hypertrophy studies frequently measure:

- Multiple muscles
- Multiple regions
- Multiple time points
- Multiple outcomes

This creates many opportunities for chance findings.

Give greater weight to:

- Pre-specified primary outcomes
- Pre-registered analyses
- Consistent effects across related measurements
- Appropriate treatment of multiple comparisons

Be skeptical when one isolated statistically significant result is highlighted among many null outcomes.

---

## 28. Pre-registration and protocol availability

Pre-registration increases confidence because it allows comparison between the analyses researchers planned and those eventually reported.

Prefer studies with:

- Pre-registration
- Registered primary outcomes
- Published protocol where relevant
- Pre-specified statistical analysis

Lack of pre-registration does not automatically invalidate a study, particularly older research.

However, unexplained discrepancies between registration and publication are a serious concern.

---

## 29. Blinding

Participants and coaches generally cannot be blinded to resistance-training interventions. This should not automatically penalize resistance-training trials.

More relevant forms of blinding include:

- Outcome-assessor blinding
- Image-analysis blinding
- Statistical-analysis blinding where applicable

For MRI or ultrasound measurements, assessor blinding is particularly desirable because measurement and image selection may contain subjective elements.

---

## 30. Conflicts of interest

Industry funding or researcher conflicts of interest do not automatically invalidate a study. They should be recorded.

Greater scrutiny is warranted when:

- The sponsor has substantial control over analysis or publication.
- Authors have strong commercial interests.
- Methodological choices systematically favour a proprietary intervention.
- Results are inconsistent with independent replication.

Evidence quality should be judged by methodology rather than assumed from funding source alone.

---

## 31. Peer review and publication status

### Normal evidence

Peer-reviewed full papers.

### Reduced confidence

Preprints and conference abstracts.

### Normally exclude

- Results available only through social media
- Unpublished claims without accessible methodology
- Marketing material
- Secondary descriptions where the primary study cannot be assessed

Preprints can be monitored but should generally receive provisional status until peer review.

---

## 32. Duplicate datasets

Multiple papers sometimes report different outcomes from the same participant cohort. These papers must not be treated as independent replications.

The project should track:

- Study cohort
- Trial registration
- Authors
- Recruitment dates
- Sample characteristics
- Intervention

When multiple publications use the same participants, they constitute one experiment, even if they generate several papers.

---

## 33. Independent replication

Confidence should increase when findings are reproduced by:

- Different research groups
- Different participant samples
- Different laboratories
- Slightly different but relevant protocols

Five publications from one laboratory should not be treated as equivalent to five independent replications.

Replication matters most when the predicted effect is small or surprising.

---

## 34. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews

Meta-analyses should be used as evidence syntheses, not automatically treated as the highest-quality evidence.

Their credibility depends on:

- Included study quality
- Population applicability
- Outcome measurement
- Appropriate pooling
- Heterogeneity
- Publication bias
- Statistical methodology
- Whether studies actually test comparable questions

The project should inspect important included studies rather than simply importing a meta-analysis's headline conclusion.

A meta-analysis containing many weak or poorly applicable experiments does not transform those experiments into high-quality evidence.

### Avoid double-counting

If a meta-analysis and its constituent studies are both examined, the meta-analysis is a synthesis of those studies, not an additional independent experiment.

---

## 35. Hard exclusion criteria

A study should normally be excluded from the hypertrophy outcome evidence pool when:

1. There is no longitudinal resistance-training intervention.
2. It reports only acute outcomes.
3. It does not measure a relevant chronic outcome.
4. The hypertrophy conclusion relies solely on a clearly unsuitable proxy such as circumference or BIA.
5. Intervention duration is too short to reasonably assess chronic hypertrophy, normally less than six weeks.
6. The paper does not provide enough methodological information to determine what participants actually did.
7. The statistical comparison required to support the claimed between-condition effect is absent and cannot be reconstructed.
8. The intervention is so heavily confounded that it cannot address the claim for which it is being cited.
9. The sample is pharmacologically enhanced when the claim specifically concerns natural lifters.
10. The publication is retracted or contains unresolved major integrity concerns.

---

## 36. Evidence downgrade criteria

The following should reduce confidence without necessarily excluding a study:

- Novice-only participants
- 8–11 week intervention
- Poorly documented training status
- Small sample
- Wide uncertainty
- Single-site hypertrophy measurement
- DXA-based hypertrophy inference
- Incomplete nutrition monitoring
- Partial supervision
- Poor adherence
- Meaningful attrition
- Unclear allocation procedure
- No assessor blinding
- No preregistration
- Poor reporting of actual training
- Baseline imbalance
- Multiple uncontrolled training differences
- Weak statistical reporting
- Findings restricted to one muscle region
- Lack of independent replication

Several moderate flaws can collectively make a study very weak even if none individually warrants exclusion.

---

## 37. Study quality classification

### Tier A — High-quality evidence

A study should generally have:

- Relevant trained population
- At least a 12-week intervention
- Strong direct outcome measurement
- Appropriate randomization/design
- Good intervention isolation
- High supervision and adherence
- Good measurement standardization
- Suitable statistical comparison
- Reasonable sample precision
- No major methodological concerns

Tier A studies should meaningfully influence project recommendations.

### Tier B — Good evidence

Generally sound study with one or several moderate limitations.

Examples:

- High-quality 10-week study
- Strong trial with single-site ultrasound
- Strong methodology but modest sample size
- Well-controlled study with imperfect nutrition monitoring

Tier B studies should influence recommendations but less strongly than Tier A evidence.

### Tier C — Weak/supporting evidence

Contains important limitations.

Examples:

- Novice-only population
- Short intervention
- Weak hypertrophy measurement
- Small and imprecise sample
- Significant intervention-control concerns
- Partial supervision
- Poor reporting

Tier C evidence can support a broader conclusion but should rarely determine one by itself.

### Tier D — Unusable for the specific claim

The study fails one or more critical requirements. It should not contribute to the recommendation for that claim.

A Tier D classification is claim-specific. A study may be unusable for one conclusion while still valid for another.

---

## 38. Do not collapse quality into a simplistic numerical score

The project may store domain ratings numerically for software purposes, but the final assessment should not rely solely on adding points.

For example:

- Excellent randomization cannot compensate for measuring the wrong outcome.
- A large sample cannot compensate for a fundamentally confounded intervention.

Certain domains therefore act as quality ceilings.

Examples:

- A novice-only population can cap applicability for advanced-lifter recommendations.
- Poor hypertrophy measurement can cap confidence in muscle-specific conclusions.
- Failure to isolate the investigated variable can cap causal confidence.
- Very short duration can cap chronic hypertrophy confidence.

The system should preserve these domain-specific limitations.

## 40. Evidence synthesis rules

### Prioritize quality over study count

Three Tier A/B studies should generally outweigh ten poorly controlled Tier C studies.

### Prioritize applicability

Evidence from trained lifters should generally receive greater weight for trained-lifter recommendations than larger quantities of novice evidence.

### Prioritize direct measurement

Direct hypertrophy measurements should outweigh indirect body-composition proxies for muscle-specific claims.

### Consider uncertainty

Do not count every study as a simple “vote.”

A study estimating `+2% ± very large uncertainty` is not equivalent to a precise estimate of `+2%`.

### Look for convergence

Confidence is highest when multiple independent, methodologically strong studies produce compatible results.

### Investigate heterogeneity

Conflicting studies should not simply be averaged away.

Differences may arise from:

- Population
- Training status
- Exercise selection
- Volume
- Effort
- Duration
- Measurement site
- Measurement method
- Intervention fidelity

Disagreement should trigger investigation rather than automatic majority voting.

---

## 41. Language used by the project

The project should match its wording to the strength of the evidence.

### High confidence

- Strong evidence indicates…
- The available high-quality evidence consistently supports…

### Moderate confidence

- The evidence suggests…
- Current evidence generally favours…

### Low confidence

- Preliminary evidence suggests…
- Limited evidence is consistent with…

### Inconclusive

- Current evidence is insufficient to determine…
- Available studies are too limited or inconsistent to support a confident conclusion.

Avoid absolute language such as “proven,” “definitely superior,” or “no difference” unless the evidence genuinely warrants that level of certainty.

---

## 42. Final decision principle

The objective of this protocol is not to find a published paper that supports a recommendation.

The objective is to determine:

> **How strongly would we believe this conclusion if we had to defend it from the underlying experimental evidence alone?**

A study receives influence according to:

```text
relevance × internal validity × outcome quality × intervention fidelity
× statistical credibility × applicability × replication
```

This expression communicates the required domains; it is not a literal unvalidated multiplication formula or a substitute for domain-specific judgement.

Weak evidence should remain weak even when convenient.

Strong evidence should be allowed to update the project even when it conflicts with an existing recommendation.

The project's scientific rules should therefore remain evidence-responsive rather than conclusion-preserving.
